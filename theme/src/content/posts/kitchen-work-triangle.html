The work triangle was designed in the 1940s around a single cook, a small American kitchen, a refrigerator, a sink and a hob. It is still taught, still drawn on brochures, and still used to justify layouts that make Pakistani kitchens harder to use. Here is why it fails here, and what we plan instead.

Why the triangle fails

The triangle connects three points — hob, sink and refrigerator — and asks that the sum of the three legs fall within a range, that no leg be shorter than about 1.2 m or longer than about 2.7 m, and that no traffic route cut through it. As an abstraction it is tidy. As a description of how people actually cook, it misses four things that matter enormously in our market.

It assumes one cook

In most of the homes we design for, two or three people are in the kitchen at once: someone cooking, someone washing, someone assembling a trolley or making tea. A triangle optimised for one person becomes a collision course for three. What you need is not one efficient loop but two or three separate work positions that can be used simultaneously without anyone crossing behind a person carrying a hot pan.

It assumes the refrigerator is the store

The triangle treats cold storage as the starting point of every journey. In practice a Pakistani kitchen holds a great deal of dry store — atta, rice, pulses, oils, spices — outside the fridge, often in a separate store room or a tall larder. If the dry store is 4 m from the prep position, the triangle's tidy perimeter is irrelevant to the trip you actually make thirty times a day.

It ignores the cooking method

A hob-and-oven model does not describe a kitchen where a tarka is finished at high heat, where a chulha-style burner or a separate gas ring is used for round-bottomed karahis, where rotis are made on a tawa at a different station, and where a large pot of stock occupies a burner for hours. These need different clearances, different ventilation and different surface heights — none of which a triangle captures.

It ignores the service side

The triangle ends at the hob. Real kitchens end at the point where food reaches a table: a plating surface, a pass, a trolley bay, and a route to the dining area that does not go through a doorway someone is about to open. In the homes we plan, the exit route and the plating zone get as much attention as the cooking zone.

Five zones instead

We plan kitchens as five zones. Each zone has its own storage, its own working surface and its own clearances, and the zones are arranged so that the most frequent journeys are the shortest.

The five-zone kitchen model
ZoneCore functionWhat must be within arm's reachTypical minimum
1. Consume Cold and dry storage, tea and beverage making Fridge, larder, kettle, cups, everyday crockery 1,200 mm of counter plus tall storage
2. Store Utensils, pans, small appliances, bulk dry goods Deep drawers under the hob, appliance garages, a walk-in or tall larder 600–900 mm of base run
3. Wash Vegetable prep, utensil washing, waste separation Sink, drainer, waste and recycling, dishwasher if present, cleaning store 900 mm sink plus 600 mm drainer each side
4. Prepare Chopping, marinating, dough, assembly An uninterrupted work surface, spice store, knife block, board storage 1,200 mm uninterrupted, ideally 1,500 mm
5. Cook Hob, oven, tawa station, high-heat finishing, extraction Two landing surfaces flanking the hob, oil and spice rail, pan store below 300 mm landing one side, 600 mm the other

The zones are not rooms and they do not have to be contiguous. In a small apartment kitchen, Wash and Prepare share a single run and Consume collapses into a tall unit. In a large family kitchen they may be on four different walls. What matters is that each zone has a working surface of its own and that the storage belonging to a zone sits in that zone — the spice rack above the prep surface, not above the sink.

The heavy-cook zone

This is the zone that the imported kitchen brochure does not have, and it is the one that decides whether a kitchen works in daily use.

High-heat cooking — a tarka, a karahi, a tawa, a long-simmering stock — produces heat, oil aerosol and smoke that a domestic European extractor is not designed to handle. We treat it as a separate sub-zone with four specific requirements:

  • Adequate capture. Extraction sized for the actual heat load, with the canopy overhanging the cooking surface on all sides by at least 150 mm, and ducting that is as short and straight as the building allows. Every bend and every metre of duct costs capture efficiency.
  • A heat-tolerant surround. No veneer, no lacquered MDF and no laminate within 600 mm of the burners. We use stainless steel, glass, porcelain or sealed stone on the splashback, and a solid or metal edge on any adjacent worktop.
  • A dedicated surface height. Standing at a tawa for forty minutes at 900 mm is uncomfortable for most people; 850 mm suits a shorter cook and a lower elbow position. Where two people cook regularly we build two heights rather than compromising on one.
  • Make-up air. An extractor that removes 900 m³/h from a sealed room creates negative pressure, which pulls air down the flue and drags odours through the rest of the house. A controllable inlet — an opening window on the opposite wall, or a trickle vent — is not optional.

Where the client wants a separate masala or utility kitchen, we plan it as a genuine second kitchen rather than a cupboard with a sink: its own extraction, its own heat-tolerant finishes, its own drainage and a door that seals, so that the display kitchen can stay in laminate-adjacent materials and look like furniture.

Two more habits from the drawing board. First, never place the hob directly beside a tall unit or a wall return — you lose landing space and you gain a grease-covered panel. Second, put the sink and the hob on the same run wherever the services allow it: it keeps the wet and the hot ends of the room together, shortens the plumbing, and leaves a clean, unbroken wall for the prepare zone.

The triangle is not useless; it is incomplete. Plan the zones, protect the clearances, and give the heavy cooking its own properly ventilated corner, and the triangle will usually fall out of the drawing by itself — which is a good sign that it was never the thing you were designing.
