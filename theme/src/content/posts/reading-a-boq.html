A bill of quantities is the only document in a construction project that tells you what you are actually buying. It is also the document most clients sign without reading, because it looks like an accounting artefact rather than a description of a building. It is not. This is how to read one.

The six sections

Almost every BOQ for an interior or fit-out project in Pakistan follows the same structure, whatever the cover sheet says. Learn the six sections and you can read any of them.

1. Preliminaries

The cost of running the project rather than building it: site management and supervision, welfare and facilities, temporary power and water, hoarding and protection, insurance, permits and approvals, security, cleaning, waste removal, scaffolding and access equipment, and the contractor's establishment charges. On a fit-out this is typically 6–12% of the build value. A preliminaries section that is suspiciously thin usually means those costs have been pushed into the measured work — where you cannot see them and where they cannot be checked.

2. Measured work

The building itself, described in quantities against a unit rate. This is the heart of the document, and it is organised by trade: demolition, masonry and partitions, ceilings, flooring, wall finishes, joinery, metalwork, glazing, painting and decorating. Each line should carry a description, a unit (m², m, nr, item, kg), a quantity and a rate.

3. Services — mechanical, electrical, plumbing

Power and containment, data, lighting, HVAC modification, fire detection and suppression, plumbing and drainage, sanitaryware. Frequently the largest single package on a commercial fit-out, and frequently the least detailed line in a weak BOQ.

4. Specialist works

Anything requiring a specialist supplier or subcontractor: acoustics, audiovisual, access control and security, signage and wayfinding, kitchen equipment, laboratory or clinical equipment, lift works, façade and external works.

5. Provisional sums and prime cost sums

Allowances for work that cannot yet be fully defined — see the next section, because this is where budgets go wrong.

6. Summary, contingency and escalation

The roll-up: subtotal, contingency, price escalation or fluctuation provisions, taxes and duties, and the tender total. This section also usually carries the validity period of the rates and the assumptions on which they were based. Read the assumptions. They are the contract's fine print.

Provisional sums explained

A provisional sum (PS) is money set aside for work that has not been designed in enough detail to measure. A prime cost sum (PC) is money set aside for a specific item or supplier whose price is not yet fixed. Both are legitimate and normal. What is not normal — and what causes most disputes — is a provisional sum that has no definition attached to it.

For every PS or PC line, four things should be written down:

  1. What scope it covers. “Feature lighting — PS” tells you nothing. “Supply and install of decorative pendants over the reception desk and the two meeting tables, including support, circuits and commissioning; excludes ceiling alterations” tells you everything.
  2. How the amount was arrived at. A budget allowance based on a comparable project, a single supplier indication, or three quotations. The basis changes how much risk you carry.
  3. The mechanism for converting it to a firm price. Who approves the final selection, within what timeframe, and against what evidence — a signed quotation, a sample approval, a shop drawing.
  4. What happens to the balance. If the PS is PKR 900,000 and the work comes in at PKR 640,000, the difference should be credited to you, not absorbed. Say so in writing.

Two further traps live in this section. The first is omission by provisional sum: work that is clearly required but has been left as a small PS rather than measured, which flatters the headline total. If the PS for joinery on a project with a documented joinery schedule is a single round number, the joinery has not been priced. The second is double-counting: the same work appearing both in a measured line and inside a PS. Checking for this takes ten minutes and finds real money.

Where risk hides

These are the places we look first when reviewing someone else's BOQ, in roughly descending order of how often they cause a problem.

Common BOQ risks and how to check for them
RiskHow it appearsWhat to do
Ambiguous descriptions “Wall finishes to living areas” with no material, no build-up, no preparation Require the specification reference on every line — a rate without a spec is not comparable
Missing preparation Flooring priced as supply and lay, with no levelling, no moisture test, no substrate repair Ask explicitly whether substrate preparation, screeding and moisture testing are included
Lump-sum services “Electrical works — item” at a single figure Insist on distribution by circuit group, containment, wiring, accessories and lighting as separate lines
Unmeasured areas Quantities derived from a leasing brochure rather than a measured survey Confirm the measurement basis and whether the area is gross, net or usable
Thin preliminaries Supervision, protection and waste removal absent or nominal Those costs exist whether they are listed or not; ask where they are carried
No exclusions list The document never says what is left out Request a written exclusions schedule and read it against the drawings
Escalation and validity Rates valid “on receipt”, no fluctuation mechanism for imported items Fix a validity period and agree in writing how currency movement on long-lead imports is handled
Client-supplied items Fittings, appliances or artwork assumed free, with no handling or installation allowance Price receiving, storage, protection and installation even where supply is excluded
Rounded quantities Every quantity a round multiple of ten Genuine measurement produces awkward numbers; heavy rounding suggests a guess
No waste allowance stated Tile, stone and veneer quantities equal to the floor area exactly Cutting waste is normally 8–15% for tile, more for patterned or diagonal layouts; ask whether it is in the rate

Notice that almost none of these risks is about the contractor being dishonest. Most are about the document not being finished. A BOQ produced from drawings that are not yet coordinated will contain gaps regardless of who wrote it — which is precisely why the design must be substantially complete before you compare prices.

Questions to ask

Take these fourteen questions to the tender meeting. They take about thirty minutes and they will tell you more than any discount.

  1. Were these quantities measured from coordinated construction drawings, or estimated from concept design?
  2. Which drawing and specification revisions is this BOQ based on? Please list them.
  3. Is substrate preparation and levelling included in every flooring and tiling rate?
  4. Are services measured by circuit and zone, or carried as a lump sum?
  5. For each provisional sum: what scope does it cover, how was the amount derived, and how is the balance credited?
  6. What is explicitly excluded? Please provide it in writing.
  7. Which preliminaries are time-related, and what happens to them if the programme extends?
  8. How long are these rates valid, and how is currency fluctuation on imported items handled?
  9. Which items are long-lead, what are their lead times, and by when must they be ordered?
  10. What is the variation mechanism — how is a change priced, by whom, and how quickly?
  11. How is the payment schedule structured, and which milestones correspond to inspectable completed work?
  12. What warranty applies to each package, and for how long?
  13. Who is the named project manager, and how often will I receive written progress reporting?
  14. What is the proposed programme, and what assumptions does it rest on — access hours, approvals, landlord constraints?

A contractor who answers these readily is one who has done the work behind the document. A contractor who becomes defensive at question five is telling you something important about how the rest of the project will run.

One last habit: compare tenders line by line, not total by total. Two BOQs for the same project can differ by 20% in total while describing materially different scopes — and the lower one is not always the cheaper one. Build a comparison sheet with the same line items down the side and each tender across the top, and the gaps become obvious in about an hour.
