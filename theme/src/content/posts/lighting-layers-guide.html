Lighting is the cheapest layer of an interior to get right and the most expensive to fix afterwards, because it is buried in the ceiling before anything else is finished. This is the four-layer method we use on every project, with the numbers we hold to and the mistakes we see most often on site.

The four layers

A room that feels comfortable almost always has all four layers present, even when nobody in it could name them. A room that feels harsh, flat or gloomy is usually missing one — most often the third.

Layer 1 — Ambient

The base level of illumination that lets you move around a space safely and see its general shape. Ambient light should be diffuse and reasonably even, without bright spots on the floor. In our residential work this is usually a cove, a plaster reveal or a large-area downlight grid at low output; in commercial work it is a linear system or a luminous ceiling. The important property is that ambient light alone should never be the brightest thing in the room — if it is, the space will feel like a shop.

Target: 100–200 lux in a living room, 300–400 lux on an open office floor, 150–250 lux in a restaurant at dinner service.

Layer 2 — Task

Light delivered exactly where work happens, and nowhere else: over a kitchen prep surface, a desk, a dressing mirror, a reading chair, a bathroom basin, a clinic examination couch. Task light is directional and controlled, and it should be switched separately from ambient so you can have task without flooding the room.

Target: 500 lux on a kitchen worktop, 500 lux at a desk for paper work, 750 lux for detailed drawing or colour-critical work, 300–500 lux at a bathroom mirror — delivered vertically onto the face, not from above the head where it casts shadows into the eye sockets.

Layer 3 — Accent

The layer that gives a room depth: light aimed at something rather than at a surface you walk on. A picture, a textured wall, a bookcase, a plant, a joinery reveal, a piece of stone. Accent lighting creates contrast, and contrast is what the eye reads as interest. A room lit only by layers 1 and 2 is measurable and dull; add accent and the same lumens feel richer.

Rule of thumb: an accent should be roughly three to five times brighter than its immediate surround to read as intentional. Below that ratio it disappears; above roughly ten times it becomes glare.

Layer 4 — Decorative

Fittings that are objects in their own right — a pendant over a dining table, a table lamp, a chandelier in a double-height volume, a wall washer grazing a plaster finish. Decorative light contributes relatively few lumens and a great deal of character. It is also the layer that most often gets value-engineered out late in a project, which is a mistake: a pendant is usually cheaper than the ceiling works it replaces.

Placement discipline: decide decorative fittings before the first-fix ceiling. Their weight, their suspension point and their circuit all need to be known while the ceiling is still open.

Colour temperature discipline

More interiors are ruined by mixed colour temperatures than by insufficient light. The rule we enforce on every project is simple: one colour temperature per space, per viewing moment.

Three specifications matter as much as the Kelvin number:

  • CRI, and R9 specifically. Colour Rendering Index above 90 is our minimum for any residential or hospitality space; above 95 for colour-critical work. R9 — the rendering of saturated reds — is the figure that separates a good fitting from a merely bright one, and it is the number most often omitted from a data sheet. A fitting with CRI 90 but R9 of 12 will make timber look grey and skin look ill.
  • Dimming compatibility. Not every LED dims, and not every LED dims on every dimmer. We specify the fitting and the dimmer as a tested pair, and we test on site at the lowest usable level — many fittings flicker or cut out below 10%.
  • MacAdam steps / SDCM. Fittings from different batches can differ visibly in tint even at the same nominal CCT. Specifying SDCM ≤ 3 and buying one batch for a visible run prevents the ceiling that looks like a patchwork.

Dimming and scenes

Dimming is not about saving electricity. It is about giving one room several personalities without rewiring it, and it is the highest-value lighting decision you can make for very little money.

We design lighting as scenes rather than as circuits. A scene is a named combination of levels across all four layers, set once and recalled by a single control.

Control hierarchy matters. Every scene must have a manual override at the wall — a physical switch that works when the app does not, when the network is down, or when a guest is staying. We specify a keypad or a rotary dimmer at each room entry, and we label the scenes on the keypad rather than leaving them as unnamed buttons. Nothing makes a well-designed lighting scheme feel broken faster than a wall of identical unlabeled switches.

Where a scheme is complex, we commission it with the client present, in the evening, with the furniture in place. Levels set on an empty room at 11 a.m. are almost always too high by 30%.

Common mistakes

  1. A grid of downlights as the whole scheme. Evenly spaced downlights illuminate the floor, not the room, and produce flat, shadowless light with dark walls. Walls that catch light make a space feel larger; floors that do do not.
  2. Spacing downlights by eye instead of by beam angle. A 60° fitting and a 24° fitting at the same spacing behave completely differently. Spacing should be derived from the beam angle and the mounting height, and the first row should sit far enough off the wall to graze it without scalloping.
  3. No lighting above the bathroom mirror, or one fitting directly above the head. This produces shadows in the eye sockets — the classic unflattering result. Two vertical sources at face height, either side, is the answer.
  4. Mixing CCTs within one sightline. A 3,000 K living room opening onto a 4,000 K kitchen reads as an error even when both are individually correct. If you must differ, differ by scene, not by fitting.
  5. Specifying lumens and ignoring glare. A bright fitting with poor shielding is worse than a dimmer one that is properly baffled. Look for the UGR rating in commercial schemes — UGR < 19 for offices, < 22 for circulation — and for deep-recessed or black-baffle trims anywhere the fitting is in the field of view from a seating position.
  6. Forgetting maintenance access. A recessed fitting in a plastered ceiling that cannot be relamped without scaffolding will be left dark. Specify accessible trims, or put the driver somewhere you can reach it.
  7. Deciding decorative fittings after the ceiling is closed. Then you inherit a pendant at the wrong position, or a rose that cannot carry the weight, or a circuit that does not exist. Decorative fittings are a first-fix decision.
  8. Lighting the room, not the surfaces. The materials schedule and the lighting schedule should be read together. A high-gloss floor, a mirrored splashback and a pale plaster wall all change how much light you need — often by 20–30% — and a scheme designed against a mood board will be wrong against the actual finishes.

One closing habit worth adopting: before any lighting is fixed, mark the intended fitting positions on the ceiling with tape and stand in the room at night with the furniture in place. It costs an hour and it catches more mistakes than any drawing review.
