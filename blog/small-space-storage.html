Storage in small apartments: eleven details that create space

From full-height runs and plinth drawers to the 600 mm rule that decides whether a corridor feels tight or just compact.

Contents
  1. Eleven details
  2. The 600 mm rule
  3. Full-height discipline
  4. What not to build
Ask a designer

Small apartments do not run out of floor area; they run out of surfaces. Every square metre of wall and every vertical metre of height is storage that most plans leave unused. These are eleven details we build into apartments between 550 and 1,400 sq ft, and one rule about depth that governs all of them.

Eleven details

1. Store in the wall, not against it

A cupboard standing in front of a wall costs floor area twice: once for the cupboard, once for the circulation you need to open it. Where the structure allows, a recessed cupboard built into a non-load-bearing partition costs nothing on the floor. On a 200 mm partition between a bedroom and a corridor, a 150 mm deep recess gives you a full-height shelved store that disappears behind a flush door.

2. Make the joinery the wall

Run a continuous joinery wall — full height, flush doors, no visible handles, finished in the same tone as the plaster. A 4.2 m run at 600 mm deep and 2.6 m high holds about 6.5 m³, which is more than most freestanding wardrobes in an apartment of that size. Because it reads as a surface rather than as furniture, the room still feels open.

3. Use the space above doors

The 400–600 mm above a door head, across a full corridor, is almost always wasted. A continuous overhead store at that level takes bedding, suitcases, seasonal equipment and archive boxes — the things you need twice a year and that consume disproportionate volume.

4. Build the bed as furniture, not as a mattress on legs

A platform bed with drawers on the long side and lift-up storage under the whole area holds the equivalent of a medium wardrobe. Where ceiling height allows, a raised sleeping platform with a full store underneath is the single highest-yield move in a small apartment.

5. Put storage on both sides of a partition

A wall between bedroom and bathroom can carry a wardrobe facing one way and a recessed cabinet facing the other, within the same 250 mm of thickness. Every partition in a small plan should be asked what it is storing.

6. Take joinery to the ceiling, always

A gap above a wardrobe collects dust and stores nothing. Closing it to the soffit adds 400–600 mm of usable depth-per-metre and removes a horizontal line that makes a low ceiling feel lower. Use a shadow gap or a minimal cornice rather than a heavy scribe.

7. Store vertically at the point of use

Kitchen: full-height pull-out larder units beside the hob, a spice column, tray dividers, and a broom store next to the door you actually use. Bathroom: a tall cabinet beside the basin rather than a mirrored cabinet above it — the tall unit holds four times as much at the same footprint. Utility: a floor-to-ceiling column with the washer, dryer, ironing board and cleaning equipment behind one door.

8. Use the inside of doors

Full-height hooks on the back of a bedroom door, a shallow rack inside a store door, a magnetic strip inside a cabinet for small metal tools. Individually trivial; together they absorb the clutter that ends up on every flat surface in the house.

9. Design for the object, not for the shelf

An adjustable shelf is a compromise. Measure the actual items — the pressure cooker, the file boxes, the shoes, the vacuum, the suitcases — and set the internal divisions to suit them. One drawer at the exact height of your tallest pan saves more space than three shelves you cannot use.

10. Fold, slide and lift rather than swing

Swing doors need a clear arc; sliding and pocket doors need none. In a corridor or a tight bedroom, a pocket door can release half a square metre. Folding doors give full access to a wardrobe interior at a fraction of the swing. Where a door must swing, hinge it so the arc falls into circulation rather than into furniture.

11. Give every surface a home for the thing that lands on it

Keys, post, bags, shoes and phones accumulate on the first horizontal surface inside the door. Build a shallow entry console with a drawer, a hook run and a shoe bench into the arrival sequence and the clutter has somewhere to go. This is the cheapest of the eleven and the one clients notice most.

The 600 mm rule

Depth is the dimension most people get wrong, because it is the one that is invisible on a plan. Our working rule: do not build storage deeper than 600 mm unless you can reach the back of it from two sides or unless it has a pull-out mechanism.

A 600 mm deep cupboard is roughly the limit of comfortable one-handed reach for most adults. Beyond that, the back third becomes a graveyard — items go in and never come out, and you end up storing things you have forgotten you own. Standard wardrobe hanging depth is 550–600 mm for exactly this reason.

Wardrobe hanging550 – 600 mmLess than 550 mm and sleeves catch on the door
Walk-in / deep store900 – 1,200 mmOnly viable with access from two sides, or with pull-out shelving on runners
Under-stairAs availableUse shallow drawers at the narrow end, deep hanging at the tall end

Where a wall is unavoidably deep — behind a soil pipe, in a structural void, beside a chimney breast — do not fill it with fixed shelving. Use full-extension pull-out drawers or a sliding tray system, so the back of the store comes to you. The mechanism costs more than the carcass and pays for itself every week.

Full-height discipline

In a small apartment, height is the only dimension you have not already spent. Three disciplines make it work.

Divide by frequency, not by convenience. The lowest 600 mm holds daily items — shoes, bags, children's things. From knee to eye level, roughly 600–1,600 mm, holds everything you use weekly. Above shoulder height, store only what you need monthly or seasonally. Getting this wrong means stepping onto a chair to reach a kettle.

Make the top accessible. A store at 2.2 m is only usable if there is somewhere safe to stand and something to hold. We keep a folding step inside the joinery itself — a shallow drawer at the base of a tall cupboard that pulls out as a step — which costs almost nothing and makes the whole upper volume practical.

Keep the top visually quiet. The upper part of a tall joinery run is at eye level from across the room. Flush doors, minimal shadow gaps and a finish that matches the wall prevent a storage wall from reading as a wall of cupboards. If it must be visible, make it deliberate — an open shelved section for books and objects, with closed storage either side.

What not to build

As important as the eleven details are the things that consume budget and floor area without storing anything useful.

  • Corner units without a mechanism. A diagonal corner cabinet with a fixed shelf is close to unusable. Either fit a carousel or a le Mans pull-out, or accept the corner as dead space and make the adjacent units deeper.
  • Open shelving in a kitchen. It photographs beautifully and collects grease. In a small apartment with limited extraction, one open shelf run above the hob will need cleaning weekly.
  • Furniture-scale storage in a tight plan. A chest of drawers, a sideboard and a bookcase in a 900 sq ft apartment consume floor area that a single built-in run would have absorbed. Choose one or the other, not both.
  • Storage under a window seat without a lift or a drawer. A hinged lid over a deep box means emptying the box to reach anything in it. Use front drawers wherever the sill height allows.
  • A walk-in wardrobe that is really a walk-through. If the only route to the bathroom passes through your wardrobe, you have built a corridor with shelves. Route circulation outside the store.
  • Over-specifying internal fittings. Velvet-lined drawer inserts and integrated lighting in every cupboard add cost without adding capacity. Spend on full-extension runners and pull-out mechanisms — those change how much you can actually use.
  • Ignoring the services. A tall cupboard planned across a soil pipe, a consumer unit or an AC drain loses 150–300 mm of depth in exactly the wrong place. Check the services layout before the joinery is drawn, not after it is installed.

The underlying principle behind all eleven details is the same: storage should be part of the architecture, not an object placed in the room. Built-in joinery that reaches the ceiling, sits flush with the wall and follows the depth rule will store more in a 700 sq ft apartment than a house full of freestanding furniture — and it will leave the floor clear, which is what actually makes a small space feel large.

Send us your plan with the wall thicknesses marked. We will show you where the recessed storage can go, how much volume a continuous joinery wall would give you, and which of the eleven details your plan is already missing.
