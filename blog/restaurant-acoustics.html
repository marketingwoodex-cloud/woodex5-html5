Why your new restaurant is too loud (and how to fix it before opening)

Reverberation time, absorption targets and the four surfaces that decide whether a full room stays at conversation volume.

Contents
  1. What loud actually means
  2. Absorption targets
  3. Four surfaces that matter
  4. Retrofit options
Ask a designer

A restaurant that is too loud loses money in ways that never appear on a complaint form: shorter dwell time, lower spend per head, fewer repeat bookings and reviews that mention the noise instead of the food. Acoustics is a design decision, not an afterthought — and it is far cheaper to solve before opening than after.

What loud actually means

“Loud” is not one thing. Three separate measures matter, and a room can fail on one while passing on the others.

Level — measured in dB(A)
The overall sound pressure in the room. A comfortable dining room sits around 60–70 dB(A) at service. Above roughly 78 dB(A) guests raise their voices to be heard, which raises the room level further — the well-known Lombard effect, where a room accelerates away from comfort once it crosses a threshold.
Reverberation time — RT60, in seconds
How long sound persists after the source stops. This is the number that decides whether a space feels live and hard or soft and calm. For a restaurant we target an RT60 of roughly 0.6–0.9 s at 500 Hz–1 kHz when the room is occupied. Above about 1.4 s speech becomes muddy and the room will feel loud even at a modest level.
Speech privacy and intelligibility
Whether a guest can follow their own table's conversation without effort, and whether neighbouring tables can hear them. Two rooms with identical RT60 can differ greatly here depending on layout, background level and how close tables sit.

Occupancy matters enormously and is routinely ignored. Every person in a room absorbs roughly 0.4–0.5 sabins of mid-frequency sound — a fully seated restaurant is measurably quieter and less reverberant than the same empty room. This is why a space that sounds perfect at a pre-opening walkthrough, tested with ten people in it, can be unbearable on the first Saturday at eighty covers.

Design against the occupied condition, and then verify it in the occupied condition. We measure on a full service night, not on the handover morning.

Absorption targets

The physics is straightforward: total absorption in the room must be high enough that reverberation time falls inside the target band. The Sabine relationship — RT60 ≈ 0.161 × V / A, where V is volume in cubic metres and A is total absorption in sabins — is enough for planning.

Target reverberation times by venue type (occupied, 500 Hz–1 kHz)
VenueTarget RT60Typical background levelComment
Fine dining0.5 – 0.7 s55 – 62 dB(A)Softest target; music should be audible but not dominant
Casual dining0.6 – 0.9 s62 – 70 dB(A)The usual hospitality compromise
Café0.7 – 1.0 s60 – 72 dB(A)A little liveness suits a daytime room with hard floors
Bar / lounge0.8 – 1.2 s70 – 80 dB(A)Music-led; speech privacy is not the priority
Open kitchen pass0.6 – 0.8 s locally—Extract noise and hard surfaces combine badly here
Banquet / events hall1.0 – 1.5 s variable—Needs adjustable absorption if the room serves both speeches and parties

A worked example: a 240 m² casual dining room with a 3.6 m ceiling has a volume of about 864 m³. To reach RT60 = 0.8 s you need total absorption A ≈ 0.161 × 864 / 0.8 ≈ 174 sabins. At full occupancy with 80 guests you already have roughly 35–40 sabins from people alone, plus upholstered seating and soft furnishings. The remaining 130–140 sabins must come from the surfaces — which is a real quantity of acoustic material, not a couple of decorative panels.

Four surfaces that matter

Acoustic treatment fails when it is applied where nobody needs it. These are the four surfaces we always look at first.

1. The ceiling

The largest uninterrupted plane in most rooms, and the one that does the most work. Options in order of typical performance per rupee: a full-area suspended acoustic raft or baffle system; a sprayed acoustic plaster over a hard soffit; a perforated metal or timber tray ceiling with an absorptive backing; acoustic panels laid into an exposed grid. A ceiling treated over 60–80% of its area will usually bring a problem room into range on its own. Exposed services look good and absorb almost nothing — do not mistake visual complexity for acoustic performance.

2. The floor

Polished concrete, porcelain and stone reflect sound and amplify chair scrape and footfall. Where a hard floor is essential to the design — and in most hospitality interiors it is — compensate elsewhere and specify acoustic pads under chair legs. A rug in a lounge area or a carpeted banquette run contributes real absorption and softens the noise floor at the same time.

3. Parallel hard walls

Two large reflective walls facing each other produce flutter echo — that bright, ringing quality you can hear by clapping once in an empty room. Breaking it does not require absorption everywhere; it requires diffusion or absorption on one of the pair. Upholstered banquettes, timber slat screens with an absorbent backing, textured plaster, or a wall of shelving and bottles all work. Bottles, incidentally, are excellent diffusers, which is one reason a well-stocked back bar helps a room sound better.

4. Glazing

Large windows and shopfront glazing are highly reflective at mid and high frequencies, and a street-facing window wall is usually the single worst surface in the room. Curtains and heavy drapes work but change the character of the space. More often we rely on ceiling treatment to compensate, and we place the noisiest tables away from the glass.

One further source that is not a surface: mechanical noise. Kitchen extraction, condensers, air handling and refrigeration can contribute 55–70 dB(A) of background level on their own — enough to make a well-treated room still feel loud. Attenuators in the ductwork, anti-vibration mounts on plant, and acoustic separation between kitchen and dining are as important as any panel. We specify these with the MEP consultant at design stage, because retrofitting duct attenuation into a live kitchen is one of the most disruptive jobs in hospitality.

Retrofit options

If you are already open and the room is too loud, these are the interventions in order of cost-to-benefit. All of them have been done on live venues without closing.

  1. Ceiling rafts or baffles above the dining area. Suspended below the existing ceiling, they add the largest quantity of absorption for the least disruption and can be installed overnight. Typically the single most effective retrofit.
  2. Acoustic panels disguised as art. Fabric-wrapped absorbers printed with graphics or photographs, mounted at the height where guests sit — 1.0–1.6 m above floor. Panels above head height do much less for speech than panels at ear height.
  3. Upholster more of the seating. Replacing hard chairs with padded seats and adding upholstered banquettes adds absorption exactly where the sound is generated.
  4. Slat screens and joinery inserts. A perforated or slatted timber screen with a mineral-wool backing reads as design, not as treatment, and breaks up a parallel wall.
  5. Table and chair feet. Felt or rubber pads on every chair. Cheap, immediate, and removes a disproportionate amount of high-frequency impact noise.
  6. Soft goods at the perimeter. Heavy curtains on a service door, a fabric-covered pin board in the pass area, rubber matting behind the bar.
  7. Manage the source. Music level and equalisation matter more than most operators expect: lower the level by 3 dB, roll off the high-mid range where speech sits, and distribute through more, smaller speakers rather than two loud ones. A single speaker at 85 dB(A) is worse for conversation than four at 72 dB(A).
  8. Control occupancy layout. Where the room cannot be treated enough, spacing tables further apart and reducing cover count on peak nights improves the experience — and, counter-intuitively, often the spend per head.

What almost never works: a single decorative panel, a plant or two, and a hope that a busy room will mask the problem. A busy room amplifies it.

Measure first. A sound level meter application on a phone is not a calibrated instrument, but it is enough to establish whether you are at 70 or 82 dB(A) on a Friday at nine, and to compare before and after. If the numbers matter commercially — and in hospitality they do — commission a proper measurement with a calibrated meter and an occupied-condition test, and design against it.

Acoustic design is included in our hospitality packages. We model reverberation at concept stage, specify treatment as part of the interior rather than as a bolt-on, and measure on a full service night before we call a venue complete.
