Our site supervision checklist: what we inspect every Friday

Twenty-two items, photographed and logged. The exact routine that keeps a nineteen-week programme from slipping in week fourteen.

Contents
  1. Why Friday
  2. The twenty-two items
  3. Photo log discipline
  4. When we stop work
Ask a designer

Quality on a site is not produced by inspecting at the end. It is produced by looking at the right thing on the right Friday, before the next trade covers it. This is the checklist our site supervisors actually carry, why the routine is weekly, and the four conditions under which we stop work.

Why Friday

A weekly rhythm beats a daily one for supervision, and Friday beats Monday in Pakistan.

Weekly, because trades need a full week to produce something worth judging. A daily walkthrough mostly photographs the same wall in three stages of incompleteness. A weekly one photographs completed work — a finished partition run, a laid floor, a sprayed door set — which is what can actually be accepted or rejected.

Friday, because it closes the reporting loop before the weekend. The report goes out the same evening. The client reads it over the weekend. Decisions are made on Monday morning rather than Monday afternoon, which is where most programmes lose half a day a week.

And because it creates a rhythm the trades learn. After three weeks every subcontractor knows that anything left unfinished on Thursday will be photographed on Friday. That knowledge does more for quality than any number of instructions issued at the start of a project.

The visit has a fixed sequence: walk the whole site once without stopping, then walk it again trade by trade with the checklist, then hold a fifteen-minute coordination meeting with the foremen about next week's sequence and access. The first walk matters — it is where you notice the thing that is not on any list.

The twenty-two items

Grouped by what is being inspected, not by who is doing it. Each item is recorded as pass, pass with note, or fail — rectify by date.

Safety and site condition

  1. Access routes clear and lit. No stored material blocking an escape route; temporary lighting working on every level.
  2. Edge and opening protection. Every floor opening, stair well and open edge guarded or covered and marked.
  3. Electrical temporary supply. Distribution boards closed, cables off the floor, no damaged leads, RCD protection tested.
  4. Fire precautions. Extinguishers present and in date, hot-work permits signed for the day, no accumulation of combustible waste.
  5. Housekeeping and waste segregation. Skip discipline — timber, metal, packaging and general waste separated; walkways swept.
  6. Protection of completed work. Floors, joinery and sanitaryware covered; protection intact and not being used as a work surface.

Setting out and structure

  1. Benchmark and datum verified. One agreed level datum for the whole floor, marked and referenced — not a different datum per trade.
  2. Setting out checked against drawings. Partition positions, door openings and joinery runs measured, not eyeballed. Tolerance on setting out is ±3 mm.
  3. Structural and landlord constraints respected. No chasing into a structural element, no drilling through a beam flange, no unapproved penetration of a fire compartment.

First fix — before anything is covered

  1. MEP containment and routes. Conduit, trunking and ductwork supported at the right centres, not resting on ceiling grid or joinery framing.
  2. Pressure and continuity tests. Water pipes pressure-tested, drainage fall verified, electrical continuity and insulation resistance tested — with results recorded, not asserted.
  3. Fire stopping and acoustic seals. Every penetration through a compartment line sealed with the specified product; resilient bars and acoustic sealant present where designed.
  4. Insulation and vapour control. Correct material, correct thickness, no gaps or compression at junctions.

Items 10 to 13 are the ones that cannot be revisited. Once a ceiling is closed or a wall is boarded, the evidence is gone. Nothing gets covered on our sites until it has been inspected and photographed — this is the single most important rule in the whole document.

Second fix and finishes

  1. Boarding and substrate. Board joints staggered, correct fixings at the right centres, no damaged or damp board, joints and fixings filled before decoration.
  2. Flooring substrate. Level within ±3 mm over a 2 m straightedge, moisture reading within the manufacturer's limit for the specified finish, cracks and screed joints treated.
  3. Joinery installation. Panels hung in the numbered sequence, gaps even, reveals consistent, doors square and latching, drawer runners full-extension and aligned, no visible fixings where the drawing says concealed.
  4. Finishes and decoration. Correct sheen and colour against the approved sample, no runs or dry spray, crisp lines at junctions, surfaces cleaned rather than painted over.
  5. Stone, tile and solid surfaces. Layout centred on the room not on the offcut, lippage within tolerance, grout colour and profile to sample, cut edges polished where visible.

Services and equipment

  1. Lighting and accessories. Correct fittings against the schedule, consistent colour temperature within a space, plates level and flush, no paint on face plates.
  2. HVAC and acoustics in place. Grilles aligned and clean, attenuators and insulation installed, plant mounted on vibration isolators.
  3. Sanitaryware and wet areas. Level, sealed, tested for leaks under running conditions, falls to outlets correct, no standing water.

Records

  1. Documentation current. Approvals received and displayed, material delivery notes and batch references filed, test certificates in hand, variation instructions signed before the work was done — not after.

Photo log discipline

A photograph without metadata is decoration. Our photo log follows five rules that make it usable as evidence rather than as a progress album.

  1. Same viewpoint, every week. Each location has a numbered camera position, marked on the plan. Standing in the same place each week is what turns a set of photographs into a time-lapse you can argue from.
  2. Wide, then close. Every location gets one wide establishing frame showing the whole area, then close frames of the specific item being inspected. A close-up of a joint with no context is not evidence of where that joint is.
  3. Caption at the time, not later. Room, item, checklist number, trade, date. Written on site while you are standing there.
  4. Photograph before covering. Every item in the first-fix group is photographed before it is enclosed, with a scale reference — a tape measure, a marked straightedge or a known component — visible in the frame.
  5. Failures get a follow-up frame. When a rectified item is re-inspected, the new photograph carries the same caption with “rectified” and the date. The log then shows the defect, the fix and the closure in one sequence, which is what a warranty claim or a dispute actually needs.

The log goes into the weekly report with three sections: completed this week, in progress with a percentage, and open items with an owner and a date. Roughly forty to ninety photographs per site visit on a mid-size commercial project — enough to be complete, curated enough to be read.

When we stop work

Stopping work is expensive and unpopular, so it needs a rule rather than a judgement call. These four conditions stop work on our sites immediately, without waiting for a meeting.

1. An immediate safety risk

An unguarded edge or opening, an unsafe temporary electrical installation, unsupported excavation or demolition, missing fire precautions during hot work, or a scaffold that has been altered by another trade. Work in the affected area stops until the condition is corrected and re-inspected. No exception, no negotiation, no “just for today”.

2. Work about to be covered that has not been inspected

A ceiling about to close over untested services, a wall about to be boarded over uninsulated penetration, a floor about to be tiled over an unverified moisture reading. This is the most common stop and the most valuable one, because the cost of opening up finished work is typically five to ten times the cost of inspecting it before closing.

3. Material that does not match the approved sample or specification

Wrong colour temperature on delivered fittings, a different stone batch, a substitute board core, a finish that does not match the approved sample panel. Installed work cannot be un-approved. Material is quarantined, photographed and held until it is verified or replaced — and the substitution question is answered in writing by the client or designer, not by whoever delivered it.

4. Work proceeding outside the approved scope or without a signed instruction

A partition being built where the drawing shows an opening, a specified item being omitted, or a variation being executed with no signed instruction. This protects the client's budget as much as the programme: undocumented work is work that will later be argued about, priced twice, or discovered during a reinstatement obligation.

Three practical notes on stopping work. First, the supervisor stops it and reports it the same hour — the decision does not wait for a manager to become available. Second, every stop is recorded in the log with the reason, the area affected, the instruction given and the time work resumed. Third, a stop is always followed by a fix date agreed with the trade foreman in writing, so that a quality decision does not silently become a programme dispute.

A site that never stops is not a well-run site. It is a site where nobody is looking on Fridays.

This checklist is standard on every Woodex project. Weekly supervised visits, a Friday photo report, an open-items log with named owners, and a written record of every inspection before work is covered. Clients receive the same document our supervisors carry — the format is in the project handover pack.
