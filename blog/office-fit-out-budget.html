What an office fit-out actually costs per square foot in Pakistan

A transparent breakdown of Cat-A versus Cat-B rates, where the money really goes, and the five line items that cause 80% of budget overruns.

Contents
  1. Cat-A, Cat-B and shell
  2. The real cost split
  3. Five overrun traps
  4. How to sanity-check a quote
Ask a designer

Every office fit-out enquiry opens with the same question: what does it cost per square foot? The honest answer is that a rate per square foot is a sanity-check tool, not a budget. This article gives you real 2025–26 bands for the Pakistani market, explains why two identical floors can differ by 60%, and lists the five line items that cause most overruns.

Cat-A, Cat-B and shell

Before any number means anything, you need to know which of three starting conditions you are pricing. Confusing them is the most common cause of a budget that is wrong by a factor of two.

Shell and core
A bare structure: concrete slab, perimeter walls, a main riser, no services distributed, no finishes. Everything below the slab is yours to build. Most new-build towers in Lahore and Islamabad hand over at this level unless your lease says otherwise.
Category A (Cat-A)
A landlord-finished, lettable space: raised floor or screed, suspended ceiling with a standard lighting grid, base HVAC installed and balanced, power to a landlord distribution board, fire detection and suppression to code, finished core and toilets. It is habitable but generic — you could not move a team in and expect them to be productive.
Category B (Cat-B)
The fit-out that makes it yours: space planning and partitions, final power and data, lighting to a design intent, finishes, joinery, meeting rooms, breakout, kitchenettes, signage, loose furniture, and the acoustic treatment that makes an open floor usable.

A Cat-B-only budget is typically 45–60% of the all-in cost of taking a shell to occupation. If a landlord quotes you a “fully fitted” rate, ask which category it covers and what is excluded — that single question has saved our clients more money than any negotiation we have ever run.

The real cost split

Where does the money actually go? On a mid-range 18,000 sq ft Cat-B fit-out we delivered in Gulberg, the split looked like this. Percentages move a little by project, but the shape is remarkably consistent.

Two observations follow from this. First, MEP dominates: a fit-out is mostly services work wearing a finishes costume. Second, the visible layer — the joinery, the flooring, the feature wall that appears in the photographs — is rarely more than a third of the spend. Clients who try to cut cost by cheapening finishes while leaving MEP untouched usually save less than they expect and lose more than they intended.

Five overrun traps

Across eleven years and roughly 90 workplace projects, the same five items cause most overruns. They are almost never the glamorous ones.

1. Base-build condition surprises

The slab is not level, the sprinkler heads do not match the new ceiling grid, the riser is smaller than the drawings imply, or the landlord's HVAC cannot handle your server room. None of this is visible in a marketing floor plan. A measured survey and a services condition report before you commit is the cheapest insurance in the industry — typically 1–2% of the build cost, against overruns that regularly reach 10%.

2. Landlord and authority requirements discovered late

Every building has its own fit-out manual: approved contractors, working hours, hoarding standards, fire-stopping details, lift booking charges, deposit and reinstatement obligations. Getting these after mobilisation means either paying to redo compliant work or paying for idle labour. Ask for the manual with the lease documents, not after you sign.

3. Long-lead items treated as ordinary purchases

Imported lighting, specialty glass, acoustic panels, branded upholstery fabric, access-control hardware and some stone can carry 8–16 week lead times, and currency movement can change the landed cost between quotation and delivery. These must be identified at design stage, priced with a firm supplier date, and ordered against a signed approval — not shopped in week twelve of a fourteen-week programme.

4. Late design changes after manufacture has started

A partition moved on site costs labour. A partition moved after the joinery for it has been cut, edged and lacquered costs the labour and the material, plus the queue time behind it. Our workshop publishes a production schedule for exactly this reason: it makes the real cost of a change visible before you ask for it.

5. Provisional sums that were never defined

A “provisional sum” line in a bill of quantities is a placeholder for work that has not yet been designed. It is legitimate — but only if the amount, the scope it covers and the mechanism for converting it into a firm price are all written down. Undefined provisional sums are the single most common route from an agreed number to a disputed one.

How to sanity-check a quote

When a proposal lands, run these seven checks before you compare prices. Two quotes that differ by 25% are frequently the same project priced at different scopes.

  1. Does it name a category? Shell, Cat-A or Cat-B. If the document does not say, the comparison is meaningless.
  2. Is the area measured or assumed? Ask whether the sq ft figure came from a measured survey or from the leasing brochure. Brochure areas can be 5–12% larger than net usable area, which quietly deflates the apparent rate.
  3. Is MEP itemised or lumped? A single line reading “electrical and mechanical works” is not a price, it is a hope.
  4. Are preliminaries visible? Site supervision, protection, welfare, insurance, permits and waste removal should appear as their own lines.
  5. What is the contingency, and who holds it? Client-held contingency is a budget control. Contractor-held contingency that never appears in reporting is a margin.
  6. Are exclusions listed? A serious proposal spends as much ink on what is not included as on what is. No exclusion list means no defined scope.
  7. Is the programme tied to the payment schedule? Milestones that correspond to completed, inspectable stages protect both sides. Payments tied to dates rather than deliverables protect nobody.

Finally, one test we would apply to any fit-out quote, including our own: can you tell from this document alone what you will have on the day the space opens? If the answer is no, the number is not a budget yet — it is an estimate of an idea.

Send us the floor plan and the lease. With a leasing brochure, a base-build description and your headcount, we can return an indicative Cat-A/Cat-B split and a realistic programme within two working days — whether or not you end up building with us.
