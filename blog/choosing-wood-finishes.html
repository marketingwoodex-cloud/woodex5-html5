How to choose wood finishes that survive a Lahore summer

Veneer, laminate, solid timber and lacquer behave very differently at 45°C and 22% humidity. Here is how we specify each one, and where we refuse to use them.

Contents
  1. The four families
  2. Where each one fails
  3. How we specify
  4. A simple decision table
Ask a designer

A finish is not a colour choice. It is a decision about how a surface will behave at 45°C, at 22% relative humidity, and again at 85% humidity six weeks later during the monsoon. Timber moves. Everything else in this article follows from that single fact.

We specify joinery for homes, offices and restaurants across Lahore, Islamabad and Karachi, which means the same wardrobe door has to survive a dry May, a wet August and a heated December. This is how we choose, and where we refuse to use a material no matter how good it photographs.

The four families

Almost every timber-look surface you will be offered in Pakistan belongs to one of four families. Knowing which family you are buying tells you more about longevity than any brand name.

1. Solid timber

Whole wood, milled and machined. In our workshop we work mostly with ash, teak, sheesham (Indian rosewood) and — for painted pieces — poplar and MDF-core solid-edge stock. Solid timber can be re-sanded, repaired and re-finished, which is why a good sheesham dining table outlives three laminate kitchens. It also moves across the grain with seasonal humidity: a 900 mm wide solid panel can move 4–6 mm between February and August.

2. Veneer

A thin slice of real timber — typically 0.5–0.6 mm — pressed onto a stable engineered core. You get the appearance and grain of the real species with a fraction of the movement, because the core does most of the structural work. Book-matched veneer, where consecutive leaves are opened like a book, is the standard for feature walls and reception desks. Veneer cannot be re-sanded meaningfully; you get one or two light refinishes at most.

3. Laminate

A printed decorative sheet bonded under high pressure to a substrate. Modern laminates are genuinely good: scratch-resistant, chemically stable, consistent batch to batch, and available in convincing timber reproductions. They are the right answer for high-abuse horizontal surfaces — hospital cabinetry, school furniture, restaurant service counters, back-of-house shelving. What laminate cannot do is be repaired. A deep scratch is permanent, and a chipped postformed edge usually means replacing the whole door.

4. Lacquer and painted finishes

Polyurethane (PU), acrylic and Duco systems sprayed onto a prepared substrate, usually MDF with sealed edges. This is where you get a specified RAL or Pantone colour, a chosen sheen level from 5% matt to high gloss, and seamless mitred returns with no visible edge band. A well-executed 20% sheen PU finish on an MDF door looks more expensive than a mid-range laminate, and it is what we specify for most residential joinery.

Where each one fails

Every family fails, and each fails predictably. Specifying well means matching the failure mode to the room.

Typical failure modes by finish family, in Pakistani conditions
FamilyMost common failureWhere it happens firstAvoid by
Solid timber Cupping, end-splitting and joint opening as humidity swings Wide flat panels facing a window; south and west elevations Keep solid panels under 300 mm wide, use breadboard ends, acclimatise stock on site for 7 days
Veneer Telegraphing of the core and edge lift where the substrate was not sealed Door edges, drawer fronts, anything near a wet area Seal all six faces, use a stable core (blockboard or good-quality ply), never veneer a bathroom splash zone
Laminate Edge band lifting and unrecoverable surface scratching Postformed edges, corners at hip height, sliding door tracks Specify ABS or laser edge banding over cheap PVC, protect corners with a solid or metal arris
Lacquer Micro-scratches visible in raking light; fingerprints on dark matt Handle-less push-to-open fronts, dark colours at grazing angles Use a 15–25% sheen rather than full matt, specify a hard-wear topcoat, add recessed pulls

Two environmental conditions in Pakistan deserve specific mention. First, the pre-monsoon heat of May and June: surface temperatures on a west-facing window reveal can exceed 60°C, which is enough to soften some adhesive systems and cause veneer edge lift. Second, the monsoon: relative humidity can jump from 35% to 85% in a fortnight, and any unsealed edge becomes a route for moisture into the substrate. Both are managed by sealing all six faces of every panel — a step that adds cost and time and is the first thing to be quietly dropped when a programme is squeezed.

How we specify

Our specification process is deliberately boring, because boring specifications do not produce surprises on site.

  1. Sample the actual substrate, not a showroom chip. A 100 mm chip of veneer tells you nothing about how a 2.4 m door will look. We press a full-size sample door in the same core, with the same edge treatment and the same number of lacquer coats, and we photograph it in the actual room at three times of day.
  2. Fix the sheen before the colour. Sheen changes how a colour reads more than the pigment does. A 10% matt clay tone and a 40% satin of the same tone look like different materials under downlights.
  3. Write the exposure into the schedule. Every line in our finishes schedule carries a location code — internal dry, internal humid, direct solar, high contact, chemical exposure. The code, not the mood board, decides the family.
  4. Specify the edge, not just the face. Doors fail at edges. We note the edge treatment on every drawing: lipped solid, ABS band, mitred return, shadow gap, metal arris.
  5. Buy one batch. Veneer leaves and lacquer batches vary. For a visible run — a wall of wardrobe doors, a reception counter — we order the whole quantity from one batch and store it together, and we number panels so the installer hangs them in sequence.
  6. Acclimatise on site. Sheet material delivered from a warehouse straight into an air-conditioned room will move after installation. We hold material on site, stickered and off the floor, for a minimum of seven days before machining.

There is one rule we do not break: no veneer and no lacquered MDF within 300 mm of a water source in a bathroom or a wet kitchen. In those zones we use solid surface, porcelain, sealed stone or marine-grade laminate with fully sealed edges. It costs more and it does not come back as a warranty claim.

If you are making these decisions without a designer, this is the short version we would give a client at a first meeting.

The single question that settles most arguments is this: can it be repaired in place? If yes — solid timber, to a degree lacquer — the material will age and can be renewed. If no — laminate, veneer — then it must be specified to a higher abuse rating from the start, because its whole life depends on the first day being right.

Bring a sample to your next meeting. If you already have a door, a worktop offcut or a piece of skirting from the space, bring it. Matching to an existing finish is a specification problem, and it is much easier to solve with the object in hand than with a photograph on a phone.
